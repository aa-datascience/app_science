## 1. Reflection Zeros Set the Scattering Invariant of Finite Majorana Wires

[arXiv:2611.17001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17001) · cond-mat.mes-hall, cond-mat.supr-con, physics.optics · emplacement **main** · texte : html, 22 477 caractères

*Pourquoi cet article :* Shows that the sign of electron reflection at zero energy counts reflectionless states in a finite Majorana wire, and that zero-bias conductance above e^2/h certifies the invariant, directly addressing the debate about Majorana signatures.

- Pourquoi c'est important : It addresses what the topological sign Q actually certifies in a finite device, where zero-bias peaks can be mimicked by ordinary bound states, and shows how transport measurements could read the invariant.
- Tags : majorana modes, topological superconductivity, scattering theory, mesoscopic transport, zero-bias conductance · difficulté 4/5

### Détail — sonnet (`claude-sonnet-5-5`)

**Vérification des citations :** vérifié — 12 citations retrouvées dans l'article, 0 introuvables (affirmations retirées).

**Explication (curieux)**

Majorana modes are exotic excitations at the ends of certain superconducting wires, proposed as building blocks for protected quantum bits. Experiments look for them as a conductance peak at zero voltage, but ordinary, non-topological states can produce the same peak. For long wires theory offers a clean test: the sign of the electron reflection at zero energy tells the two phases apart. In a real, finite wire, though, it was unclear what that sign actually certifies.

This paper shows that the sign is set by counting special states called reflectionless states, in which a wave enters through one contact and leaves entirely through the other. These states have complex energies. The sign is negative when an odd number of them sit in the lower half of the complex energy plane. For a finite wire this means the two Majorana end modes are split by less than their coupling to the contacts. It does not mean the wire is in a protected topological phase.

In a symmetric device, each sign change is a point where two such states merge, which makes the transmission as flat as possible around zero energy. The sign is also readable from conductance: at zero temperature it is negative exactly when the zero-bias conductance at a contact exceeds one unit of $e^2/h$. This works only if the temperature is low enough; the paper's example Kitaev chain at 45 mK is too warm.

**Explication (physicien)**

Setup: a wire with BdG Hamiltonian $H$ (even dimension, class D) is coupled to leads L and R in the wide-band limit. The reflection determinant factorizes as $$\det r_L(E)=\frac{\det(E-H_Z)}{\det(E-H_P)},\quad H_{P,Z}=H\mp\tfrac{i}{2}\Gamma_L-\tfrac{i}{2}\Gamma_R.$$ Poles are eigenvalues of $H_P$, where both leads drain the wire. Zeros are eigenvalues of $H_Z$, where L acts as gain and R as loss; they are reflectionless states.

Key result: in a Majorana basis $H=iA$ with $A$ real antisymmetric and $\Gamma_{L,R}$ real, so $H_{P,Z}=iM_{P,Z}$ with real $M$. Then $\det r_L(0)=\det M_Z/\det M_P$. Since both leads drain the wire and $N$ is even, $\det M_P>0$, so $Q=\operatorname{sign}\det M_Z$. Zeros are symmetric under $z\to -z^*$. Off-axis pairs contribute $|z|^2>0$, while each zero on the negative imaginary axis contributes a negative factor. Hence $$Q=(-1)^{N_-},$$ with $N_-$ the number of zeros with $\operatorname{Im}z<0$. $Q$ changes only when a zero crosses $E=0$ along the imaginary axis. With one lead, zeros are conjugates of poles, so $Q=+1$.

Two-Majorana model: $$z_\pm=\tfrac{i}{2}(\gamma_L-\gamma_R)\pm[\varepsilon^2-\tfrac14(\gamma_L+\gamma_R)^2]^{1/2},$$ and $\det M_Z=\varepsilon^2-\gamma_L\gamma_R$, so $Q=\operatorname{sign}(\varepsilon^2-\gamma_L\gamma_R)$. For $\gamma_L=\gamma_R=\gamma$ a real zero pair meets at $E=0$ at $\varepsilon=\gamma$ and splits along the imaginary axis. This is an EP of $H_Z$, since $H_Z$ is nilpotent there. Particle-hole symmetry ($z\to-z^*$) plus a mirror symmetry ($z\to z^*$) force sign changes to occur as double zeros at $E=0$. This gives $|\det r_L|^2\propto E^4$ and $$T(E)=\frac{4\gamma^4}{E^4+4\gamma^4},$$ a second-order Butterworth response. For $\gamma_L\ne\gamma_R$ the sign change at $\varepsilon=\sqrt{\gamma_L\gamma_R}$ is separated from the EP at $(\gamma_L+\gamma_R)/2$.

Conductance: if lead L couples through one Majorana mode, $$G_{LL}(E)=\frac{e^2}{h}[1-\operatorname{Re}\det r_L(E)],$$ so at $T=0$, $Q=-1$ exactly when $G_{LL}(0)>e^2/h$. In the two-mode model $G_{LL}(0)=(e^2/h)\,2\gamma_L\gamma_R/(\varepsilon^2+\gamma_L\gamma_R)$.

Numerics: a 1D InAs/Al wire ($L=3\ \mu$m, $B_c=0.864$ T) and a 2D Kwant device. The first sign change is at $B_1=0.8619$ T in the wire, with $1-T_1\propto E^4$ (fitted exponent 3.99). Seven further sign changes per device occur as $\varepsilon$ oscillates. Eq. (2) is checked at all 384 wire and 741 2D settings. The threshold requires $k_BT\lesssim0.2\gamma$ and a single Majorana coupling mode. Quasi-Majorana states with weakly coupled modes violate the temperature condition.

**Contexte**

- Avant : For long wires, the sign of $\det r(0)$ was known to be the class D topological invariant, changing only where a mode is perfectly transmitted. For finite wires, earlier work showed $Q=+1$ through one lead and $Q=\operatorname{sign}(\varepsilon^2-\gamma_L\gamma_R)$ between two leads, so its meaning depended on contacts. Scattering poles and conductance peaks were well studied, and reflection zeros and reflectionless EPs were known in photonics and in Josephson junctions.
- Ce que l'article change : The paper gives the finite-wire invariant a physical meaning as the parity of the number of reflection zeros in the lower half-plane. It shows that mirror-symmetric devices have every sign change, including the bulk transition, as an EP of reflectionless states with $E^4$ flat transmission. It also derives a conductance criterion: $Q=-1$ exactly when the zero-bias conductance exceeds $e^2/h$ (at zero temperature), and gives a fitting route at finite temperature.
- Limites : These are my own assessments, not statements of the authors, except where noted. The results rest on numerical models (a single-subband wire, a Kwant 2D strip, a Kitaev-chain model) and on no measured data. The conductance threshold needs one Majorana mode coupled to the lead and low temperature; the paper itself says the 45 mK Kitaev experiment is too warm and quasi-Majorana states with smooth barriers break the condition. The authors state that $Q=-1$ does not prove a protected phase or fix its gap, and that quasi-Majorana states can mimic it. Mirror symmetry is needed for the EP to coincide with the sign change, and disorder separates them; retuning one barrier restores the double zero in only 38 of 48 wires. Finite-temperature extraction of $Q$ was tested only on synthetic spectra.

**Explication (curieux)**

Majorana modes are proposed zero-energy states at the ends of superconducting wires, and they are candidates for storing quantum information. Experiments have struggled because ordinary trapped states can produce the same zero-voltage conductance peak. This paper asks what a transport measurement through a finite device can actually certify about its topology. The author builds on a known sign, usually called Q, which is set by how electrons are reflected at zero energy. He shows that this sign counts special resonances, called reflection zeros. A reflection zero is a state that accepts an electron from one contact and passes it entirely to the other contact, sending nothing back. Q is negative when an odd number of these zeros lie on one side of the energy plane. In a finite wire, a negative Q means the two end Majorana modes are split by less than their coupling to the contacts. With symmetric contacts, each change of sign happens when two zeros merge at zero energy, which gives perfectly flat transmission. The paper also argues that the sign can be read from the zero-bias conductance, since Q is negative exactly when that conductance exceeds one conductance quantum, e squared over h, at zero temperature. The author notes that this does not prove a protected phase, and that quasi-Majorana states can mimic the signal.

**Explication (physicien)**

The paper studies a finite superconducting wire of Bogoliubov-de Gennes Hamiltonian H, in class D, attached to normal leads L and R with wide-band couplings. The scattering matrix is S(E) = 1 - 2 pi i W^dagger (E - H + i Gamma/2)^{-1} W, with time dependence exp(-iEt/hbar), so resonances (poles) lie in the lower half-plane. The reflection block of lead L satisfies det r_L(E) = det(E - H_Z)/det(E - H_P), where H_{P,Z} = H minus or plus (i/2) Gamma_L, minus (i/2) Gamma_R. Poles are eigenvalues of H_P, where both leads drain the wire. Zeros are eigenvalues of H_Z, where lead L feeds the wire like a gain and lead R drains it.

The central counting rule uses a Majorana basis, where particle-hole symmetry makes H = iA with A real antisymmetric and the Gamma matrices real. Then H_{P,Z} = iM_{P,Z} with real M matrices. At E = 0, det r_L(0) = det M_Z / det M_P. The pole factor carries a fixed positive sign, because both leads drain the wire. Zeros z = i lambda are symmetric under z to -z*. Off-axis pairs contribute positive factors |z|^2, while each zero on the negative imaginary axis contributes a negative factor. Hence Q = sign det r_L(0) = (-1)^{N_-}, where N_- counts zeros with Im z < 0. Q changes only when a zero crosses E = 0 along the imaginary axis.

For two Majorana modes with splitting epsilon and rates gamma_L, gamma_R, one obtains det M_Z = epsilon^2 - gamma_L gamma_R, so Q = sign(epsilon^2 - gamma_L gamma_R). With mirror symmetry, two zeros meet at E = 0 at each sign change, forming a double zero. The two-mode transmission is T(E) = 1 - |det r_L(E)|^2 = 4 gamma^4/(E^4 + 4 gamma^4) for equal couplings, a second-order Butterworth response. The local conductance satisfies G_LL(E) = (e^2/h)[1 - Re det r_L(E)] when lead L couples through one Majorana mode. At zero temperature, Q = -1 exactly when G_LL(0) exceeds e^2/h.

Numerical checks use an InAs/Al single-subband wire of length 3 micrometres, with bulk critical field B_c = 0.864 T, and a 2D Kwant device. Equation (2) was checked at 384 wire settings and 741 2D settings. The first sign change occurs at B_1 = 0.8619 T in the wire, and 1-T_1 scales as E^4 with fitted exponent 3.99. The authors note that unequal couplings separate the sign change from the exceptional point, and that disorder can do the same.

*Pourquoi cet article :* Reports a gate-tunable superconducting quantum point contact in Ge/SiGe where switching-current quantization directly tracks conductance quantization, a clear few-mode supercurrent result matching the reader's Josephson and mesoscopic interests.

### Détail — sonnet (`claude-sonnet-5-5`)

**Vérification des citations :** vérifié — 11 citations retrouvées dans l'article, 0 introuvables (affirmations retirées).

**Explication (curieux)**

A quantum point contact is a very narrow constriction that lets only a few electron 'lanes' pass. When it connects two superconductors, a current can flow with no voltage, carried by a few discrete channels. The authors build such a device in germanium-based layers (Ge/SiGe), using electric gates to squeeze the constriction and open lanes one at a time. They place the constriction in a loop with a much larger reference junction (a highly asymmetric SQUID). Sweeping a magnetic flux through the loop then reveals how the supercurrent depends on the phase difference across the constriction. The maximum supercurrent rises in steps that match the steps in normal-state conductance, so each opened channel adds its own share of supercurrent. The shape of the phase dependence lets the authors estimate how transparent each channel is, and they can tune this with the gate. The measured steps are about 5 nA, well below the ideal value of about 18 nA for a perfectly transparent channel. The authors attribute this to imperfect transmission, thermal effects and the way the junction switches, and they cannot separate these contributions. They also shine microwaves on the device. Depending on gate voltage they see different behaviors, including photon-assisted Andreev processes and Shapiro-like patterns, and the phase dependence changes in ways a simple adiabatic picture does not explain.

**Explication (physicien)**

Setup: Ge/SiGe hole-gas heterostructure with PtSiGe superconducting contacts. A gate-defined SQPC (500 nm long, 100 nm gate width, device A) is embedded in a strongly asymmetric SQUID with a large reference junction (500 nm by 1.4 µm) whose switching current reaches 1.5 µA, so the reference junction's phase is approximately fixed and flux modulation of the switching current probes the SQPC CPR. Reference junction switching current is subtracted, measured with the SQPC depleted.

Normal-state characterization at 700 mK shows three conductance plateaus at integers of $2e^2/h$ with subband spacing 2 meV. At base temperature the zero-bias conductance exceeds $50\cdot 2e^2/h$ but remains finite, which the authors attribute to a high phase escape rate, so I_c cannot be read from I-V curves. The induced gap is extracted from conductance peaks near pinch-off ($\Delta^*\approx 55\ \mu$eV) and from MAR peaks at $V_G=-0.6$ V, fitted with $\Delta^*(T)=\Delta^*_0\tanh(1.74\sqrt{T_c/T-1})$, giving $\Delta_0^*=76\ \mu$eV and $T_c=550$ mK.

CPR analysis: the half peak-to-dip amplitude $\Delta I^*_{SW}$ shows three plateaus matching the conductance plateaus. The CPR is fitted with a zero-temperature short-junction sum over three ABS, with $$I_{ABS}(\varphi)=\frac{e\Delta}{2\hbar}\frac{\tau\sin\varphi}{\sqrt{1-\tau\sin^{2}(\varphi/2)}}$$ and a gate-independent amplitude scale factor. The unconstrained fit yields one, two and three contributing modes on the successive plateaus, with transmissions increasing within each plateau. Supplementary fits give transmission above 75% per mode. The authors regard extracted $\tau$ as lower bounds. Measured steps are about 5 nA versus $e\Delta/\hbar\approx 18$ nA. They note that $\tau=0.8$ would reduce the step to about 55% of the ideal value, with thermal ABS occupation and switching dynamics as further contributions. The CPR amplitude and skewness decrease with temperature, more strongly in the single-mode regime; measurements are limited below 200 mK. Switching statistics of the reference junction are not described by standard underdamped models, so no correction is made.

Microwaves (device B): the reference junction shows integer and half-integer Shapiro steps at 2.25 GHz. The SQPC shows power-dependent MAR splitting on the slope of the first plateau, zero-bias power dependence on the first and second plateau, and Shapiro replicas at low bias in the high-transmission regime. The CPR under irradiation at 2 and 11.5 GHz is compared with the adiabatic model $\phi(t)=\phi_0+2\alpha\cos(2\pi ft)$, $I_{ad}=\sum_n J_0(2n\alpha)I_n\sin(n\phi)$. It reproduces the amplitude reduction but not the skewness evolution or the extra features near $\phi=\pi$, suggesting non-equilibrium ABS occupation.

**Contexte**

- Avant : Ballistic supercurrent quantization had been studied in atomic break junctions and gate-defined SQPCs, with critical-current steps analogous to conductance quantization. Ge/SiGe had emerged as a platform with highly transparent contacts, Andreev-enhanced conductance quantization, and microwave compatibility.
- Ce que l'article change : The paper combines a Ge/SiGe SQPC with an asymmetric SQUID to read out the CPR, shows switching-current plateaus matching conductance quantization, and extracts gate-tunable effective transmissions of individual modes. It also reports microwave-driven MAR, Shapiro features and CPR changes beyond the adiabatic approximation.
- Limites : The measured steps (about 5 nA) are well below the ideal value (about 18 nA), and the authors cannot separate finite transmission, thermal ABS occupation and switching dynamics. The switching current underestimates the intrinsic critical current, and the switching statistics are not reproduced by standard models, so no quantitative correction is made. Fits use a zero-temperature short-junction model with three modes and a gate-independent scaling factor, so the transmissions are lower bounds and model-dependent. Measurements are limited below 200 mK. The microwave CPR changes are only qualitatively attributed to non-equilibrium ABS occupation, without a quantitative model. Gate hysteresis and shifts between cooldowns also affect comparisons. These are partly my own remarks as a referee, beyond what the authors state.

## 4. Circular phonons generate negligible magnetization in insulators

**Explication (curieux)**

When a crystal's atoms are driven to move in circles by circularly polarized infrared light, the moving ions act like tiny current loops and might make the material magnetic. Earlier experiments on materials such as SrTiO3 saw a huge rotation of the polarization of a probe light beam and read it as a very large magnetization. But the same rotation can also arise from a purely optical effect, in which pump and probe light mix in the crystal without any magnetism, so the rotation alone cannot settle the matter. The authors tested this in silicon carbide (6H-SiC). They measured the rotation inside the driven crystal, then did something different: they placed a thin gallium phosphide crystal right next to the driven crystal to act as a magnetic-field sensor. A real magnetization would leak a magnetic field outside the crystal, whereas the optical mixing effect would not. Inside the driven SiC they saw a sizable rotation, which could be read as anything from about 0.3 tesla down to a few microtesla depending on assumptions. Outside, they saw no field within their sensitivity. From this they conclude that the magnetization is below about a millitesla. Their conclusion is that a large rotation in an insulator does not imply a large magnetization, consistent with the original small theoretical estimates.

**Explication (physicien)**

Setup: 6H-SiC (35 µm, c-cut) is driven by ~500 fs circularly polarized pulses at 25.8 THz, inside the Reststrahlen band of the doubly degenerate E1 mode (23.9 to 29.8 THz). Pump polarization purity is better than 95%. The Faraday-like rotation of an 800 nm probe is measured in reflection from the back surface (double pass), averaged over probe polarization angle to remove pump-induced linear birefringence, and half-differenced between pump helicities.

Ambiguity: The antisymmetric part of $\chi^{(3)}$, via the four-wave mixing process $\omega=\omega-\Omega+\Omega$, gives an off-diagonal $\Delta\epsilon_{ij}\propto E^*\times E$ once Kleinman symmetry breaks near the phonon resonance. It has the same symmetry as a magnetic term $\Delta\epsilon_{ij}=\alpha_{ij}^{mag}M_z$. Helicity and fluence dependence therefore cannot separate the two (SI S1). The peak rotation is ~3 µrad with FWHM ~700 fs. Using the equilibrium Verdet constant (19 rad/T/m at 800 nm), this corresponds to $B_{eq}\approx0.3$ T, so $\mu_0M\approx0.3$ T if one follows Ref. 4. Treating the phonons as an applied field instead gives $\mu_0M\approx|\chi_{SiC}|B_{eq}\approx4$ µT, with $\chi=-1.3\times10^{-6}$ from SQUID data. The rotation inside the driven volume therefore cannot fix $M$.

Stray-field magnetometry: A wedged ~60 µm GaP(100) Faraday detector (Verdet constant ~120 rad/T/m) sits on the unpumped side of the SiC, so only the double pass through the GaP is analysed. The SiC is thick enough to block the pump, and a linear-pump control showed no GaP birefringence signal. The pump fluence was ~40 mJ/cm². Noise is ~5 nrad per point after ~110 h of averaging, within a factor of unity of shot noise. Result: no helicity-dependent stray field above ~1 µT (10 nrad of rotation).

Calibration: COMSOL time-dependent Maxwell simulations model a magnetized disk with diameter equal to the pump beam and thickness equal to the penetration depth. The magnetization is axial and follows the measured temporal profile. The stray field is averaged over the forward and return probe paths in the GaP. This gives a calibration of GaP rotation versus peak $\mu_0M$. The result is $\mu_0M<1$ mT, and 300 mT is excluded. For a magnetization peaking at 2 mT, the field peaks around 800 nT. Varying the assumed depth among 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75 µm rescales the bound by no more than a factor of two.

Conclusion: the bound is more than two orders of magnitude below the 0.3 T estimate, so large helicity-dependent rotation does not imply large magnetization.

**Contexte**

- Avant : Circular phonons were reported to produce large effective magnetic fields or magnetizations, for example in SrTiO3, where Faraday rotation implied magnetizations roughly four orders of magnitude above ionic-current estimates. Theory predicts small orbital moments of about 10^-4 Bohr magneton. Separately, it was argued that the nonlinear optical susceptibility $\chi^{(3)}$ can mimic Faraday rotation without magnetism, so rotation measured inside the driven volume was ambiguous.
- Ce que l'article change : It adds a direct, model-independent test: ultrafast stray-field magnetometry with a GaP Faraday detector next to the driven crystal. The result supports the small theoretical estimates and shows that rotation inside the sample cannot calibrate magnetization.
- Limites : The result is for one material, 6H-SiC, an ordinary polar insulator, and one drive frequency. It does not directly show that the large signals in SrTiO3, KTaO3 or LiNbO3 are non-magnetic; the title's generality ('in insulators') is an extrapolation. **Explication (curieux)**

When ions in a crystal move in circles, they can form tiny current loops, which might produce a magnetic moment. Recent experiments on several materials reported large rotations of light's polarization inside laser-driven crystals and read these as evidence of strong induced magnetism. This paper tests that reading. The authors drove silicon carbide with circularly polarized mid-infrared laser pulses that set its atoms moving in circles. They measured a large polarization rotation inside the crystal, but the same rotation could be explained either by a very large magnetization or by a much smaller one, depending on assumptions. The decisive test was to look outside the crystal. A magnetic field produced by a real magnetization would leak out into the surrounding space, while the optical effects that mimic magnetism stay inside the illuminated region. Using a thin gallium phosphide detector placed right next to the sample, the team saw no magnetic stray field above about one microtesla. Simulations then bound the induced magnetization to less than one millitesla, far below the roughly 0.3 tesla implied by the internal measurement. The result suggests that large polarization rotations in driven insulators do not, by themselves, indicate large magnetization. The method is also presented as a more reliable way to measure magnetization in driven solids.

**Explication (physicien)**

The paper addresses whether circularly polarized excitation of an infrared-active phonon in an insulator produces a sizeable true magnetization. The test material is 6H-SiC, driven at room temperature on its doubly degenerate E1 mode with circularly polarized pulses centred at 25.8 THz (about 500 fs long, 11.6 µm wavelength). The pump spectrum lies within the Reststrahlen band, and the reflectivity shows a band from 23.9 THz to 29.8 THz.

Two measurements are combined. First, the polarization rotation is measured inside the 35 µm thick crystal with an 800 nm probe that is reflected from the second surface, so it traverses the photoexcited region twice. The probe polarization angle is scanned and averaged to remove pump-induced linear birefringence and dichroism, and the helicity-dependent signal is the half-difference between opposite pump helicities. The peak rotation is about 3 µrad with a full width at half maximum of about 700 fs. The authors point out that the signal contains a four-wave-mixing term proportional to the antisymmetric part of the third-order susceptibility, which mimics a Faraday response with a sign that follows pump helicity. Interpreting the signal with the equilibrium Verdet constant gives an equivalent field of about 0.3 T, while treating the phonon as an applied field gives a magnetization of about 4 µT.

Second, a 60 µm GaP (100) Faraday detector is placed 5 µm above the unpumped face of the SiC, wedged so that only the reflection from the face nearest the sample is analysed. The detector measures the double-pass rotation, with a noise level of about 10 nrad, corresponding to a field uncertainty of about 1 µT. No pump-induced stray field is resolved. Finite-element Maxwell simulations (COMSOL, cylindrical symmetry) model the photoexcited region as a homogeneous disk of pump diameter and penetration-depth thickness, with magnetization along the pump direction and a time profile matched to the measured dynamics. Averaging the field over the detector volume gives a calibration curve, from which the authors infer an upper bound of about 1 mT on the magnetization, compared with the 300 mT expected for the Faraday-equivalent interpretation. Varying the assumed penetration depth changes the bound by at most a factor of two.
